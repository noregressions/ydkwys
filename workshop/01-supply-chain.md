# Part 1 — What Is Actually in Our Software?

Start the machine check now. It runs while we talk, and `build-all.sh` is the
slowest step in the whole workshop:

```command
./scripts/tools-check.sh
./scripts/build-all.sh
```

`tools-check.sh` reports the version of every tool the labs need and flags
anything missing or too old. `build-all.sh` pulls the base images, builds
S01–S05, and warms the scanner databases. If either fails, the workshop
container has all of it inside already — see *Getting Started*.

## The supply chain is bigger than your dependency list

Ask a room what their software supply chain is and you get the direct
dependencies: the things in `pom.xml` or `package.json` that somebody chose
on purpose. That list is real, and it is the smallest part of the answer.

Everything below puts code into your build, your artefact, or your running
process:

- **Direct dependencies** — chosen deliberately, reviewed occasionally.
- **Transitive dependencies** — chosen by the people who wrote your direct
  dependencies, reviewed by nobody in your organisation.
- **Build plugins and their own dependencies** — Maven plugins, Gradle
  plugins, bundler plugins. These execute arbitrary code during your build,
  in a dependency graph that is not your application's dependency graph.
- **Build backends and lifecycle hooks** — PEP 517 backends that construct a
  Python package during `pip install`, npm `prepack` and `postinstall`
  scripts that run before or after the tarball exists.
- **Build engines and runtimes** — Maven, npm, the JDK, Node, CPython. All
  of them ship code that ends up shaping or entering the artefact.
- **Base images and OS packages** — an entire second distribution's worth of
  software, arriving one `FROM` line at a time.
- **The support state of all of the above** — whether anyone upstream is
  still fixing any of it.

Every one of those is a decision somebody else made on your behalf, and you
ship the consequences.

## The one question

Every lab in this manual follows a few named dependencies — the **tracked
components** — from the line that declares them to the process that runs
them, asking the same thing at each boundary:

```text
is it still identifiable here?
```

Not *is it still there* — it usually is. The code survives the build almost
every time. The question is whether anything downstream can still tell you
it is there. That gap between presence and identifiability is the subject of
the workshop, and it turns out to be where most of the interesting failures
live.

## Three things to ask at every boundary

When you look at any inventory — a dependency tree, an SBOM, a scanner
report, a container listing — ask:

1. **Evidence.** What evidence says this component is present?
2. **Identity.** What attributes identify it: coordinates, hashes, metadata?
3. **Boundary limits.** What could be present here that this evidence source
   is structurally incapable of showing?

Question three is the one people skip, and it is the one that decides whether
a clean report means anything.

## How this is arranged

The manual is four acts, plus the setup you have already done and the
appendices you take home.

**Act 1 — You can't see what you ship.** *(Parts 1–2)* We put six real builds
through their real toolchains and watch named components stop being
identifiable: shaded and relocated in Java, bundled in JavaScript, generated
by a Maven plugin, generated by an npm hook, generated by a Python build
backend, and translated between registries in a Jakarta EE application. We
also find the one evidence source that recovers some of it. Then we point a
commercial scanner at the hardest case and watch it fail for a reason that no
amount of cleverness fixes.

**Act 2 — The scanner can't either.** *(Parts 3–4)* Given that inventories
are incomplete, we look at what happens to the vulnerability data laid over
the top. A real CVE record read end to end, over the six years it kept
changing its mind. Then the three ways the data misleads you — tool
capability gaps, CVE process failures, and the end-of-life blind spot —
brought together on a single component.

**Act 3 — Someone is counting on that.** *(Part 5)* The gaps in Acts 1 and 2
are not just inconvenient; they are an attack surface, and there is
documented history of each one being used. We look at the mechanisms, then at
what actually closes them: code-level scanning, controlled ingress, and an
artefact that can prove where it came from.

**Act 4 — The minimum that keeps you honest.** *(Part 6)* The short,
repeatable drill you run every release so that nothing in the ship list is a
surprise. You run it here, on a real project, and take the command card home.

```text
Part 1   What is actually in our software?
Part 2   Can we identify what we ship?
Part 3   What does a CVE finding actually mean?
Break
Part 4   CVEs aren't enough
Part 5   Someone is counting on that
Part 6   The minimum that keeps you honest
```

Work through them in order. If a lab misbehaves, move on rather than debug it
— every one has its captured output in its lesson, and the point of each is
visible in a single command.

## Terminology

These words get used loosely in the industry and precisely in this manual.

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| declaration | The dependency you requested, in a manifest or POM |
| resolution | The concrete versions the solver actually chose |
| build-time software | Code that executes during the build: plugins, backends, hooks |
| transformation | A structural change to code: shading, relocation, bundling, minification |
| artefact | The packaged binary you distribute: JAR, WAR, tarball, wheel, image |
| inventory | What some producer says is present: an SBOM, a scanner report, a tree |
| identity | The handle a tool matches on: PURL, CPE, coordinates, checksum |
| provenance | Evidence of where an artefact came from and how it was built |
| vulnerability | An advisory record correlated against an identity |
| lifecycle / EOL | Whether anyone upstream is still committed to fixing it |

The distinction that does the most work is **declaration versus artefact**.
Almost every tool in the industry reads one of the two, reports confidently,
and does not tell you which one it read.

## You should now be able to

- Name at least four ways code enters your build that are not in your
  dependency list.
- State the difference between a component being *present* and being
  *identifiable*.
- Say which boundary any given inventory was generated at.
